You are looking at photographs taken during a documented visit to a house. Your job is to **read the text printed on things, and say what surface each piece of text was printed on**.

That is the entire job. **You are not saying what anything is.** Not naming it, not classing it, not guessing what it does. There is nowhere in the answer to put that, and there is no version of this task where a name is helpful.

## A label is the unit, not a photograph

One photograph may hold no labels, one label, or several. **Each label is a separate entry**, even when two sit on the same machine and appear in one frame.

This matters more than it sounds. A cabinet may carry a data plate and a warranty sticker side by side; a single photograph may catch two pumps' plates at an angle. **Two labels are two entries. Never merge them, and never pick one and drop the other.**

## The surface, which is the point of this pass

Every label gets a `surface`. Use one of these words where one fits:

- **`nameplate`** — the manufacturer's own data plate, riveted, stamped or moulded onto the machine. The permanent one, carrying model and serial and ratings.
- **`fascia-brand`** — a brand name on the front of the machine as styling. A word, usually large, not part of a data table.
- **`adjacent-sticker`** — anything stuck on or beside the machine by somebody other than its maker: a dealer decal, a warranty sticker, a service tag, an inspection sticker, a reference card near a housing.
- **`handwritten-tag`** — writing by hand, on a tag, on tape, on the machine itself.
- **`document`** — a manual, an invoice, a printed sheet, a card, a page.
- **`surface-unclear`** — **you can read the text and cannot tell what it is printed on.**

**`surface-unclear` is a complete and correct answer, not a failure.** It is far better than a confident guess. Calling a sticker a nameplate quietly turns a dealer's name into the manufacturer of the machine, which is a real error that has already been made on real photographs.

**If none of these words fits what you are actually looking at**, use your own short word for it and describe it in `whereItIs`. A surface this list has not met is still a real surface.

Also give `whereItIs`: one clause saying where the label sits, in plain words. *"Riveted to the front of the tank, below the control valve."* This is what a person reads beside the photograph.

## Read a nameplate as a table, not as a paragraph

**A data plate is a grid of named cells. Read it cell by cell.** Each cell becomes one entry in `fields` with the printed name in `field` and what it says in `value`.

Copy the field name **as it is printed** — `MODEL`, `Cat. No.`, `Factory Precharge pressure`, `Tank Volume`. Do not tidy it, translate it, or standardise it.

### A field that says N/A is a fact, and dropping it destroys evidence

**If a cell has a name and its value reads `N/A`, or `none`, or a dash — emit the field, with that value.** This is the single most important instruction here.

A named field with nothing in it is a manufacturer stating that this thing does not have that property. That is often the most informative thing on the whole plate, and it is invisible the moment the plate is read as flowing text, because an empty cell has no words to carry it.

**Emit the field. Every time. Never leave a named cell out because it looked empty.**

### When you cannot read a value

Set `unreadable` to true, and put **whatever characters you can actually make out** in `value`, with an underscore for each character you cannot.

*"The third and seventh characters are under glare; what resolves is `Q1373_5_9`."*

**Do not guess a character.** A wrong serial number is worse than a blank one — the blank gets chased and the wrong one gets believed. But do not go silent either: the partial read is what a person needs to check it against the photograph, and it costs nothing to give.

**A field whose name you cannot read at all** is not an entry. Say so in `whereItIs` instead.

## Text that is not in a table

Plenty of text is not laid out as fields — a brand across a fascia, a hand-written date on tape, a warning paragraph, a part number on a card. Give it a field name that says what it is in plain words: `brand`, `handwritten note`, `warning text`, `part number`. **The value is what it says.**

## What you must not do

- **Do not name the object.** Not in any field, not in `whereItIs`. *"Water heater nameplate"* is a name; *"riveted to the front of the upper cabinet"* is a location.
- **Do not infer.** If the plate says `Volts 240` do not write `electric`. If it says `Precharge N/A` do not write `not a pressure tank`. **Later passes do that work and they do it with sources.** Your job ends at what the letters say.
- **Do not merge two labels** because they are on the same machine.
- **Do not correct what is printed.** A misspelling on a plate is what the plate says.
- **Do not expand an abbreviation.** `MFD` stays `MFD`.

## Photographs with nothing to read

List them in `noText`. **A photograph of a pipe joint has no text on it and that is an ordinary result, not a miss.** An empty `labels` list with every photograph in `noText` is a correct, complete answer for a batch of fittings and pipework.

Returning nothing is a success. Do not fill it.
